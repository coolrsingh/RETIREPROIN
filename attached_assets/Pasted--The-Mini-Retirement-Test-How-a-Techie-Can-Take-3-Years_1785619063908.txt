Every few months, a version of this conversation happens in a Bengaluru cafe or a Pune WFH slack huddle.

*"I've got the idea. I've got a co-founder. I've got maybe 30 lakh saved. I just don't know if I can afford three years without a salary."*

And then nothing happens. Not because the idea was bad. Because the question stayed vague. "Can I afford it?" is not a question a spreadsheet can answer — it's a feeling, and feelings at 2 AM always vote no.

Here's the reframe that turns it into an answerable question:

**A sabbatical is a mini-retirement.**

Financially, it's identical. Income stops. Expenses continue. You either live off a corpus or you don't. The only difference is that a mini-retirement is temporary and you're doing it at 34 instead of 60.

Which means the tool that answers "can I retire?" is the exact same tool that answers "can I take three years to build this?"

## Why "how much runway do I need" is the wrong first question

Most people planning a startup break calculate one number: monthly expenses × months of runway.

Say your household burn is ₹1.1 lakh a month. Three years is ₹39.6 lakh. Add a buffer, call it ₹45 lakh. You have ₹30 lakh. Conclusion: not yet, save more.

That calculation isn't wrong. It's just answering the smallest question in the room.

The bigger question is what those three years cost your 60-year-old self. Because runway money is money you *spend* — you feel it, you can count it, you can top it up later. The retirement damage is money you *never invested*, and that number is much larger and completely invisible.

## The hidden cost: three years of paused SIPs

Let's put numbers on it.

Take a 34-year-old engineer investing ₹60,000 a month across equity mutual funds, planning to retire at 60. She pauses everything for three years to build her startup.

**What she didn't invest:** ₹21.6 lakh. Feels manageable.

**What that would have become by 60:** roughly **₹2.8 crore.**

*(Illustration assumes an 11% annualised return, monthly compounding, contributions resumed unchanged after the break. Actual returns will differ — this is arithmetic, not a forecast.)*

Read those two numbers again. ₹21.6 lakh of missed contributions, ₹2.8 crore of missing corpus. The gap is the compounding those particular rupees would have earned over 26 years — and they were your *earliest* rupees, the ones with the most time to work.

This is the number nobody calculates before they resign. Not because it's hard, but because nobody thinks to look 26 years out when they're staring at 36 months of runway.

## The number that actually decides it

Now here's the part that flips the story.

If she resumes investing at 37 and increases her SIP by roughly **₹22,500 a month**, she lands back in approximately the same place at 60.

*(Same 11% assumption, 23 years of contributions from age 37.)*

That's the real decision. Not "can I afford three years off" — but **"after the break, can I sustain a SIP that's about ₹22,500 higher than before?"**

For a senior engineer returning to a ₹50L+ role, or a founder whose company found some traction, or someone who steps back into consulting at a higher rate — that's a very different conversation from "you'll never retire."

And if the answer is no? Then you now know something concrete. Maybe the break is two years instead of three. Maybe you keep a ₹20,000 SIP running through the break, which changes the catch-up requirement dramatically. Maybe you retire at 62 instead of 60. These are *dials*, and you can only turn dials you can see.

## Running your own mini-retirement scenario

This is exactly what RetirePro was built to model — a plan, not just a number. Here's the sequence:

**Step 1 — Build your baseline.** Age, current corpus, monthly SIP, expected retirement age, current monthly expenses. Save this projection. This is your "stay employed" scenario and everything gets compared against it.

**Step 2 — Model the break.** Reduce contributions to zero (or to whatever you'll realistically maintain) for the sabbatical years. Then resume at the level you honestly expect post-break — not a fantasy number.

**Step 3 — Subtract the runway.** Your ₹45 lakh of living expenses during the break comes *out of* investable savings. Many people forget this and double-count the same money as both runway and retirement corpus. Reduce your starting corpus accordingly.

**Step 4 — Compare the two saved projections.** You'll see the corpus gap at 60 and, more usefully, whether your retirement income still covers your retirement expenses. That's the number that matters. A ₹2.8 crore gap on a ₹12 crore requirement is a serious problem. The same gap when your plan already had comfortable headroom is a rounding error you can close with one good year.

**Step 5 — Turn the dials.** Two years instead of three. A ₹15,000 SIP maintained through the break. Retirement at 62. Watch which lever moves the needle most — the answer is often not the one you expected.

## If you're married with kids, the picture changes shape

A single 30-year-old taking a startup break is playing with their own future. A 36-year-old with a four-year-old is playing with someone else's, and that deserves harder math — not vetoes, just harder math.

Add the child goals explicitly:

**Higher education.** A course costing ₹25 lakh today, needed in 15 years, at 8% education inflation, works out to roughly **₹79 lakh**. That's not a distant abstraction — it's a bill with a due date, and unlike retirement, you cannot postpone it or take a loan against your own future.

**Everything else.** School fees rising every year, a second child, a wedding fund if that's part of your plan, ageing parents who may need support. Each one is a dated liability sitting between you and 60.

Model these as separate goals with their own timelines, and the sabbatical question sharpens: does the break push a corpus shortfall into the exact year your child starts college? Because a retirement gap at 60 has 26 years of runway to fix. An education gap in year 15 does not.

Two practical points for anyone doing this with a family:

- **Health insurance.** The day you resign, your corporate cover ends — and it was probably covering your spouse, children and possibly parents. Buy an independent family floater *before* you resign, while you're still an employed applicant with clean paperwork. The premium is a permanent new line item in your household budget, so put it in the plan.
- **Runway and emergency fund are not the same money.** Runway funds the startup years. The emergency fund handles the medical event or the family crisis that happens *during* those years. If one pot serves both, one bad month ends the venture. Keep six months of household expenses fenced off and untouchable.

Also worth doing: a joint conversation, both partners looking at the same projection. Startup breaks strain marriages far more often through unspoken financial anxiety than through actual money problems. A shared, visible number removes the guessing.

## What "the number isn't shaking" actually looks like

You'll know your plan survives the break when three things are true:

1. **The retirement projection still meets your target income at your target age** — with the break modelled and the runway subtracted.
2. **The catch-up SIP is realistic** given the income you can plausibly earn afterward, whether the startup works or not.
3. **Every dated goal in between still gets funded** — education, home loan closure, parental support — without borrowing against the retirement corpus.

Hit all three and the honest answer is: *go build the thing.* You've stress-tested the downside and it holds.

Miss one and you haven't been told no. You've been told which variable to change first.

## The reframe worth keeping

Most people treat the startup dream and the retirement plan as enemies — as if pursuing one means abandoning the other. So they either leap without looking, or they never leap at all and spend the next twenty years wondering.

Both of those come from the same root problem: **not knowing the number.**

The most expensive assumption in Indian personal finance isn't a bad fund choice or a mistimed market entry. It's assuming you're on track without ever having calculated it. That assumption keeps some people in jobs they've outgrown, and it sends others into ventures they genuinely couldn't afford.

Run the numbers and you get something better than confidence. You get *permission* — or a specific, fixable reason why not yet.
