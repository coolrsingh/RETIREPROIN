# The ₹40 Crore Illusion: Why India's Wealthiest Retirees Are the Most Exposed

*Suggested slug: /blog/real-estate-rich-retirement-illusion*
*Suggested meta description: Most Indian HNIs believe their net worth guarantees a comfortable retirement. Here's the quiet arithmetic that says otherwise — and what to do about it.*

---

Picture a man in his late fifties. Two flats in Mumbai, a commercial unit he rents out in Thane, a plot back in his hometown he's been meaning to sell "when the price is right," a portfolio of mutual funds and FDs, and a net worth statement that reads ₹40 crore. On paper, he is not just comfortable. He is done. Retirement, if he wanted it tomorrow, looks solved.

It isn't. And the reason has nothing to do with how much he's worth. It has to do with what that worth is *made of*, and what it will be asked to survive.

This is the story of two blind spots that, individually, are manageable — and together, quietly break more HNI retirement plans in India than market crashes ever do.

## Blind Spot One: Being Rich on Paper, Poor on Cash

Real estate isn't just a large slice of Indian HNI wealth. It's the dominant one. Industry surveys of India's high-net-worth population consistently find that somewhere between a third and half of total wealth sits in residential and commercial property — and in a recent large-scale survey of affluent Indians, over half reported allocating more than the recommended threshold to real estate alone. This isn't a fringe habit. It's the default.

The reasons are completely rational. Property has been the most trusted store of value across generations of Indian families. It's tangible. It's a hedge against inflation. It carries social weight — a house isn't just an asset, it's a statement about the family's standing. None of that is wrong.

What it isn't, is *liquid*. And liquidity is the one thing retirement actually runs on.

Here's the arithmetic nobody puts on a slide: Indian residential real estate typically yields 2–3% in annual rental income against its market value. Compare that to a retirement corpus that needs to comfortably throw off 6-8% a year in withdrawals to fund a genuinely high-consumption lifestyle. A ₹20 crore property empire sitting inside a ₹40 crore net worth isn't generating retirement income — it's generating a rental cheque that barely covers the property tax and society maintenance on the *next* property.

So what happens when the plan needs cash — a medical emergency, a child's overseas education fee, simply funding twelve months of living expenses? The property doesn't liquidate in a week. It doesn't liquidate in a quarter. Selling a ₹5 crore flat at a fair price, in India, without taking a haircut for urgency, routinely takes six months to over a year — and that's assuming the market isn't soft when the need arises, which it frequently is, precisely because emergencies and market downturns tend to travel together.

This is the part almost nobody accounts for: **the net worth number and the spendable number are not the same number**, and for most Indian HNIs, the gap between them is enormous.

## Blind Spot Two: The Corpus That's Being Eaten Alive Quietly

Now overlay the second problem, because it doesn't operate on its own — it compounds with the first.

Every retirement projection assumes some rate of expense inflation. Most Indian retirement calculators — including, frankly, most financial advisors — use a blended household inflation assumption of 5-6% and call it done. That number is roughly right for groceries, fuel, and utilities.

It is nowhere close to right for healthcare.

Medical inflation in India has been running at 12-14% annually for the past several years, against a general CPI closer to 4%. That's not a rounding difference — it's triple the rate. At that pace, healthcare costs don't just rise, they *double roughly every five to six years*. A procedure that costs ₹10 lakh today plausibly costs ₹20 lakh in five years and approaching ₹40 lakh in ten — well within a single retirement.

The insidious part is exactly how invisible this is. Nobody receives a monthly notice saying their health cover lost real value this year. A ₹50 lakh sum insured bought a decade ago felt generous at the time. Measured against today's private hospital ARPOB (average revenue per occupied bed), which has itself been climbing 10-16% a year as hospitals lean into premium, high-margin procedures, that same ₹50 lakh now covers a shrinking fraction of what it used to. Senior citizens in India are already spending, on average, close to a fifth of their income on healthcare — and that share is only going to widen as the population ages and treatment sophistication (and cost) rises with it.

For an HNI, the instinct is often "I'm wealthy enough to self-insure — I don't need to worry about this the way a middle-class family does." That instinct is exactly backwards. Wealthy families tend to opt for exactly the tier of care that's inflating fastest: private rooms, accredited super-specialty hospitals, robotic and AI-assisted procedures, second opinions, sometimes treatment abroad. The richer the lifestyle, the *higher* the effective medical inflation rate actually experienced — not lower.

## Where the Two Collide

Here's the scenario that plays out more often than it should, and it's the reason these two topics belong in the same conversation rather than two separate blog posts.

A family's retirement plan looks fine because the net worth number is large. Then a medical event arrives — a parent, a spouse, sometimes the retiree themselves — and the bill, inflated at three times the rate anyone budgeted for, is far larger than expected. The liquid portion of the portfolio — the mutual funds, the FDs — gets drawn down faster than planned. It isn't enough. So the family looks at the property.

And now they're selling real estate under duress: a compressed timeline, often a soft market (because emergencies rarely arrive on a convenient economic cycle), for an asset that never generated meaningful income while it sat there. They lose on the sale price. They lose the years of "it'll appreciate eventually" they were counting on. And the healthcare bill that triggered it all was inflating the entire time the sale process dragged on.

This is not a market crash. This is not bad luck. This is two extremely common, well-documented, entirely foreseeable patterns in Indian HNI wealth — an illiquid, real-estate-heavy net worth and a chronically underestimated healthcare inflation rate — meeting at exactly the wrong moment. It happens to families who did everything that "felt" responsible: they bought property, they bought some health insurance, they built a mutual fund SIP. What they didn't do was stress-test the *shape* of their wealth against the *timing* of when they'd actually need it.

## What an Honest Plan Looks Like Instead

None of this is an argument against owning property, or a case for panic. It's a case for precision. A retirement plan built for genuine HNI circumstances — not a template scaled up — does a few specific things differently:

**It caps real estate as a share of investable net worth**, not as a share of total net worth. A primary residence is a lifestyle asset, not a retirement asset — it shouldn't be counted toward the corpus that's supposed to fund your monthly expenses, because you can't spend a house you're living in.

**It builds a liquidity ladder**, not a liquidity hope. That means holding enough in genuinely liquid instruments — not "liquid within a year if the market cooperates," but liquid within days — to cover a minimum of 12-24 months of expenses plus a dedicated medical contingency, separate from the general emergency fund.

**It inflates the healthcare line item honestly.** Not at 5-6%, but closer to 12-14%, compounded across the full retirement horizon. And it reviews sum insured every 2-3 years rather than assuming a policy bought a decade ago still does its job — because at this rate of inflation, it almost certainly doesn't.

**It treats a property sale as a planned event, not an emergency lever.** If part of the strategy genuinely is to unlock real estate value in retirement, that unwind should be scheduled and modeled years in advance — not discovered as the only option when a bill lands unexpectedly.

The families who get this right aren't the ones with the largest net worth. They're the ones who stopped treating the net worth number as the answer, and started asking a sharper question: *if I needed ₹50 lakh in cash next month, where would it actually come from — and how would that answer change during a market downturn?*

That question, more than any single number on a net worth statement, is the real test of whether a retirement plan works.
